What ad agencies actually do

Everyone and their grandma seems to be opening a marketing agency. Here is what they sell, what it costs, how to spot a bad one, and how to do much of it yourself. Every fact links to its source.

The short answer

An agency rents you a marketing team: a strategist, an ads specialist, an SEO person, a writer or designer, and an account manager who sends the monthly report.

For most small businesses the monthly work comes down to four things: keep the Google profile and website in shape, run Google and/or Meta ads and cut wasted spend every week, make content (posts, ad videos, pages), and report the numbers. None of it is magic. Most of it is steady, repeatable work, which is why it fits into prompts.

Why everyone is opening one

It's cheap to start

  • No stock, no premises: a laptop and some software. Google and Meta ads are self-serve, so anyone can open an account.
  • Retainers mean recurring monthly income from each client.
  • "SMMA" (social media marketing agency) became a popular get-rich pitch, with courses and videos teaching it. Many articles about it are themselves selling a course, so treat income claims with caution.

What that means for you

  • Selling is the main skill in many new agencies. Insiders writing on Medium say the founder's real job is finding clients.
  • A lot of work is outsourced. Many small agencies resell "white-label" SEO, ads or web work done by a partner under their own brand, with a markup. Fine if they're open about it; ask who does the work.
  • Local clients buy results, not "Facebook ads". Calls and bookings are what matter, and they're harder to attribute than online sales.

What it costs

The best public data is for SEO. Ahrefs polled 439 SEO providers:

$3,209average monthly retainer for agencies
$1,349average monthly retainer for freelancers
~$99/haverage agency hourly rate

Ads management is priced in several ways: a flat monthly fee, a share of ad spend, or both. Ad spend comes on top: it goes to Google or Meta. Google's third-party policy requires anyone managing your Google Ads to disclose their fees and give you the cost, click and impression data.

Do it yourself, or hire?

Do it yourself first when

  • Your ad budget is small. A $800 monthly fee on $500 of ads means 62% of your marketing money pays the manager.
  • You're local and the work is mostly your Google profile, reviews and website. That's free, and nobody knows your business better.
  • You can spare 2 to 4 hours a week. A playbook and an AI chat do the writing; you supply the judgment.

Consider hiring when

  • Ad spend is big enough that the fee is a small share (the same $800 on $5,000 of ads is 14%).
  • You need specialist skills you won't use often: tracking setup, video production, a complex store feed.
  • Your time is worth more running the business. A middle path: pay an expert for a one-off audit or setup, then run it yourself.
